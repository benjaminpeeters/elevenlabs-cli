I was twenty-six the first time I saw Sanaa from the air. The plane came in low over the mountains and the whole city seemed to be built of gingerbread, brown and white, with the light going gold behind it. I remember thinking that I had never seen anything so old and so alive at the same time.
The clinic was two rooms and a courtyard. We had a generator that worked when it felt like it, a stock of gauze that never lasted the month, and a head nurse called Amal who could set a broken arm faster than I could find the splints. She taught me most of what I know about patience.
Winter there is not what you imagine. The days are bright and the nights are bitter, and the women would come in wrapped in three shawls, carrying babies wrapped in four. We would warm our hands over the stove before touching anyone. That was the rule. Cold hands frighten a child.
I have thought about that rule for forty years. It is not really about hands. It is about noticing what the other person will feel before you do the thing you came to do. Most of nursing is that. Most of life, I suspect.
When the news comes on now and I see those same streets, I do not see a war. I see Amal's courtyard, and the stove, and a girl of six who sang to her little brother while I stitched his knee. She would be a grandmother now, if she is anywhere at all.
So when a young man puts up a poster about Yemen on a Tuesday morning, I go. I bring a tin of biscuits and I listen. It is a small thing. But small things, done every time, were the whole of the work back then, and I do not believe that has changed.
