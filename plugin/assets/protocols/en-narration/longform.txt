The first thing to understand about a tide is that it is not a wave. A wave is water moving; a tide is the whole ocean leaning, twice a day, toward a moon that is never in the same place two hours running. Along a straight coast the lean is gentle and predictable. In a narrow bay it is anything but.
The bay we are concerned with is forty kilometres long and, at its head, less than two wide. Water that enters at the mouth has nowhere to go but up, and so it does: the difference between low and high water here is the largest recorded anywhere, sixteen metres on a spring tide, the height of a five-storey building.
People have lived on this shore for at least four thousand years, and for all of that time the tide has been both larder and threat. At low water the flats stretch to the horizon, and a person who knows them can gather a week of food in an afternoon. At high water the same person, in the same place, would be under fifteen metres of cold sea.
The knowledge of when to go out and when to turn back was passed from parent to child, and it was exact. Not roughly right: exact to the quarter hour. The records of the parish, kept from the sixteen hundreds, list forty-one drownings in three centuries, and nearly all of them were strangers.
The engineers arrived in the nineteen sixties with a different kind of knowledge. They saw sixteen metres of water rising and falling twice a day and calculated what a turbine could take from it. The numbers were enormous. The plans were drawn, the models were built, and then, for reasons that had nothing to do with engineering, nothing happened for fifty years.
What happened instead is the subject of this series: how a community that had always lived by the tide came to argue about owning it, and what they decided in the end.
